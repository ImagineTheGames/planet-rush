The report
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  "other players shots down follow the diretion they were fired in"

The brief's scope note stands: the host-choppiness half of the original report
moved to a0-75 once the developer established the stutter follows the PC and not
the host. This audit is shots only.


1. WHAT THE WIRE CARRIED FOR A SHOT — ESTABLISHED FIRST, CHANGED SECOND
================================================================================
BEFORE (wire v2, src/net/snapshot.ts @ 40608f6) — 6 bytes, no heading anywhere:

    id u8 | posX i16 | posY i16 | meta u8

    id    the POOL SLOT, not an entity id. Slots are recycled.
    pos   eighths of a world unit (POS_SCALE).
    meta  owner in bits 0..2, ship-vs-turret kind in bit 3, bits 4..7 reserved.

A shot's velocity was not on the wire in any form — not a vector, not an angle,
not an index. `applySnapshot` said so in as many words ("Velocity is not streamed
for shots — six bytes each, and a client that sees the whole board can watch them
fly"), and the interpolation buffer said it twice more ("with no velocity on the
wire there is nothing to extrapolate *along*").

AFTER (wire v3, this branch) — 8 bytes:

    id u8 | posX i16 | posY i16 | heading u8 | speed u8 | meta u8

    heading  a full turn over one byte — 1.406° per step, ±0.703°.
    speed    world units/s in steps of 4 — 0…1020 u/s, ±2 u/s.

Polar, not a velocity vector, because a shot is a straight line at a constant
speed and those are exactly its two degrees of freedom: 2 bytes rather than the 4
an i16 pair would cost. The polar step is the codec's own business, like
POS_SCALE — `ProjSnap` carries a plain `velX`/`velY` in world units on both sides
of the socket, so no consumer knows the wire is polar.

WIRE_VERSION 2 → 3. Mandatory, not cosmetic: the record STRIDE moved, so a v2
reader would walk a v3 projectile array two bytes out of step per entity and
decode the whole stream into garbage rather than reject it.


2. HOW A REMOTE SHOT ADVANCED BETWEEN SNAPSHOTS — BEFORE
================================================================================
Only ever by packet. There were three playback regimes and exactly one of them
moved a shot at all:

  interpolate  the render clock falls between two buffered snapshots that both
               hold this slot: lerp the chord between the two positions.
  hold         render clock older than the oldest snapshot: DRAW IT WHERE IT IS.
  extrapolate  render clock newer than the newest (the stream stalled):
               DRAW IT WHERE IT IS.

and inside `interpolate`, a slot present in only one of the two frames — a shot
just fired, or one that landed mid-bracket — was also DRAWN WHERE IT IS.

So the answer to the brief's second question is: **neither dead reckoning nor,
strictly, interpolation — a remote shot was positioned by snapshot and by nothing
else, and stood still whenever two snapshots did not happen to bracket it.**

The chord itself was fine. Measured against the ray the sim actually fired each
shot along, an interpolated dot sat 0.026 u off it — the wire's own eighth-unit
position step, and nothing more. THE DIRECTION WAS NEVER WRONG WHILE PACKETS
FLOWED. What was wrong is that a shot only travelled its line while they did.


3. THE ERROR, IN WORLD UNITS
================================================================================
Two errors, because they mean different things and only one of them is visible to
a cross-track measurement:

  cross-track  perpendicular distance from the drawn dot to the ray (muzzle,
               heading) the sim launched it on — "is it on its line at all".
  along-track  how far the dot is from where the shot TRULY was at that instant,
               measured along its own line — "is it where its heading put it".

A frozen shot has a cross-track error of ZERO. It is on its line; it is not
travelling it. To a player reading that line to decide whether to move into it,
the two are the same defect, and along-track is the one that names it.

                                                      BEFORE        AFTER
  duel — one shooter, empty field, clean wire
    along-track  mean / max                       0.304 / 8.698  0.023 / 0.075
    frozen render frames                              16 / 476        0 / 476

  field — 8 ships firing into 40 rocks, clean wire
    along-track  mean / max                       0.326 / 8.748  0.026 / 0.084
    frozen render frames                            180 / 5018       0 / 5018

  field + 200 ms of lost snapshots
    along-track DURING the loss  mean / p95 / max
                             29.592 / 95.315 / 104.042  0.028 / 0.057 / 0.070
    frozen render frames                            299 / 4969       0 / 4969

  field + 400 ms of lost snapshots
    along-track DURING the loss  mean / max
                                     24.702 / 104.042    0.027 / 0.078
    cross-track max  (frames off their own line)
                              34.682  (20 frames)  1.133  (4 frames)

  field + ±15 ms arrival jitter
    frozen render frames                             93 / 5018       0 / 5018
    along-track  mean                                   3.209          3.125

Read the four numbers that matter:

  * 8.7 u — EVERY SHOT, EVERY MATCH, on a perfect wire. One frozen render frame
    per shot (180 frozen frames for 180 shots), at its first appearance: the dot
    sat at the muzzle for a bracket and then jumped 8.7 u. That is the frame a
    player reads to decide whether the line is coming at them.

  * 104 u — a 200 ms gap in the stream. The shot stopped dead and finished the
    gap 104 world units behind where its own heading had it: a THIRD of the ship
    weapon's entire 300-unit reach, and then a 104-unit teleport when the packets
    came back. A player who moved out of the frozen dot's way was moving into a
    shot that was never where it was drawn. This is the fairness defect, and it
    is what a mobile radio, a lossy wifi link or a backgrounded tab produces.

  * 34.7 u — the crab, and it is real but it is NOT the main event. Under a
    400 ms loss the buffer prunes down to two frames and the recycled-pool-slot
    guard (`maxShotStep`, ~38 u at a 33 ms span) stops being able to tell two
    shots apart: 20 render frames drew a dot up to 34.7 u OFF the line the shot
    was fired on, travelling between two shots that were not the same shot. It
    needed packet loss to appear, so it is not what the developer saw on every
    volley — the freeze is.

  * 1.133 u — the honest cost of the fix. Cross-track error goes UP, from 0.084 u
    to 1.133 u worst case, and every unit of that is the one-byte heading
    quantization showing up exactly where it was predicted to: at the far end of
    the longest reach the buffer will take (200 ms × 700 u/s = 140 u, and
    140·tan(0.703°) = 1.7 u is the bound). On the ordinary 33 ms reach it is
    0.171 u. The shot's collision radius is 5 u.


4. WHAT CHANGED
================================================================================
  src/net/snapshot.ts       ProjSnap carries velX/velY; the record is 8 bytes;
                            SHOT_ANGLE_SCALE and SHOT_SPEED_QUANT are the two
                            quantizations and their error budgets are written out.
                            WORST_CASE_BYTES 494 → 622.
  src/net/wire.ts           WIRE_VERSION 2 → 3 (the record stride moved).
  src/net/interpolation.ts  every regime advances a shot along its own heading.
                            `hold` and `extrapolate` are one signed operation now
                            (`flyShot`), capped by MAX_SHOT_EXTRAPOLATION_MS =
                            200 ms — five times the ship cap, because a ship can
                            thrust and turn inside the reach and a shot cannot.
                            A singleton inside a bracket flies too: forward for a
                            shot that landed, backward for one just fired.
                            The recycled-slot guard gained the sharpest test it
                            could have — a shot's muzzle vector is constant, so
                            two records of the same shot encode identically and
                            two different shots agreeing to within 1.4° AND
                            4 u/s is a coincidence, not a match.
  src/net/presentation.ts   the presented pool slot is written with the wire's
                            velocity (and it is stashed and restored, so the
                            picture still never reaches the simulation).
  src/net/prediction.ts     applySnapshot writes projectile.vel. It did not, and
                            the pool is slot-keyed with `takeProjectile` handing
                            out the LOWEST free slot — so a wire shot inherited
                            the muzzle vector of whatever shot used that slot
                            before it, and every replayed tick carried it along
                            that stale line.


5. WHAT THIS COSTS ON THE WIRE
================================================================================
Per shot, per snapshot: 2 bytes. At the 30 Hz broadcast rate that is 60 B/s while
a shot is in flight, and over a whole flight:

  a stock ship shot   0.577 s ≈ 17 snapshots ≈  35 bytes, once, ever
  a turret shot       0.343 s ≈ 10 snapshots ≈  21 bytes, once, ever

Per client, measured (not extrapolated) on the 8-ship rock-field scene above,
which averaged 8.7 concurrent shots and peaked at 14:

                                     BEFORE      AFTER
    mean snapshot payload            162.4 B     179.9 B    (+10.8 %)
    peak snapshot payload              194 B       222 B
    downstream at 30 Hz             5.17 KB/s   5.68 KB/s   (+0.51 KB/s)

Per client, worst case (the GDD §4.2/§4.3 entity caps — 8 ships, 64 shots):

                                     BEFORE      AFTER
    snapshot payload                   494 B       622 B    (+26 %)
    downstream at 30 Hz            14.88 KB/s  18.63 KB/s   (+3.75 KB/s)
    vs the 40 KB/s per-client budget      37 %        47 %

With the economy channel's measured ~5 KB/s on top (docs/netcode-spike.md §7) a
worst-case client sits at ~24 KB/s, 60 % of budget, from 50 %. The day-0 decision
(30 Hz, 512 B snapshot budget) is unaffected in kind; the measured worst case is
now 622 B and docs/netcode-spike.md has been re-billed against it rather than
left to drift. src/net/snapshot.test.ts pins the 622 so it cannot.

Two bytes and not one: heading alone would be 1 byte, and a client can recover
speed from two positions a heading has confirmed are the same shot. It is not
enough. The frame a shot FIRST appears on has no history to recover anything
from, and that is the frame the whole mechanic turns on — a player reads a new
line to decide whether to move into it (GDD §2.6). "A shot arrives, snapshots
stop, and it continues" is only satisfiable from one packet.


6. WHAT IS STILL TRUE AFTER THIS, AND IS NOT MINE TO FIX HERE
================================================================================
Arrival jitter still warps a shot's apparent SPEED along its line. At ±15 ms the
per-frame step ranges 0 → 22.5 u against a true constant 8.67 u, so a shot surges
and eases as packets bunch. The fix took the frozen frames in that scene from 93
to 0 and left the along-track mean where it was (3.209 → 3.125 u).

That is not a shot problem and it must not be fixed as one: `bracket()` locates
the render clock in ARRIVAL-TIME space, and remote ships ride the same bracket
through the same warp. The snapshot carries an exact `tick`, so bracketing in
tick space instead would remove it for both — one contained change to one method,
affecting every interpolated entity. Named here as a follow-up rather than
smuggled into a shots-only brief.

The shot is on its line throughout (cross-track mean 0.026 u under that jitter);
what varies is how fast it travels it.
